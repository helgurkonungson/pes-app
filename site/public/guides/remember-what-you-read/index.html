How to remember what you read: 6 research-backed techniques

A week after finishing a book, most people can retell a couple of ideas from it. That's not bad memory — it's how we read. Here are six techniques that are well studied in cognitive psychology and work for books, articles and textbooks.

Why we forget what we read

In 1885 Hermann Ebbinghaus measured how fast he forgot what he had learned and found the forgetting curve: most is lost in the first hours and days, then forgetting slows down. Every successful recall makes the curve flatter.

The second reason is the illusion of knowing. When you reread, the text feels familiar and the brain mistakes familiarity for understanding. The test is simple: close the book and try to explain the chapter in your own words.

1. Recall instead of rereading

The most reliable technique is retrieval practice, also called the testing effect. In a well-known study by Roediger and Karpicke (2006), students who tried to recall a text after reading it remembered noticeably more a week later than students who spent the same time rereading.

How to use it: after each chapter, close the book and write down 3–5 main ideas from memory. Then check the text and add what you missed.

2. Review with growing gaps

The same review time pays off more when it's spread over days. This is the spacing effect. Come back to the key ideas after a day, then a few days, a week and a month. More in the guide on spaced repetition.

3. Explain it in your own words

If you can explain an idea simply, you understand it. If you stumble, you've found a gap. That's the core of the Feynman technique. Answering "why?" and "how does this connect to…?" helps more than just retelling.

4. Read in small portions

Memory starts with attention: what you skim half-distracted is never encoded. Fifteen focused minutes without your phone beat an hour in the background. A little every day works better than three hours once a week.

5. Interleave topics

When you mix similar topics instead of studying them in blocks, the brain has to work out which approach each one needs. It feels harder and less productive, but it sticks better — especially for problems and skills.

6. Connect it to what you already know

New information holds better when you hook it to something familiar: an everyday example, your own experience, another book. Ask yourself: where have I seen this? How is it different from what I knew? Where will I use it this week?

FAQ

How many times should I reread a book to remember it?

Rereading on its own helps little. Read carefully once, then recall the main points from memory a few times with growing gaps: after a day, a few days, a week and a month.

Do notes help?

Yes, if you write in your own words and from memory rather than copying. Turning notes into questions and answering them later helps even more.

Can I remember a book reading 10–15 minutes a day?

Yes. Short daily sessions that end with recall work better than rare long ones. Consistency and self-testing matter most.
