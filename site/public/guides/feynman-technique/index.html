The Feynman technique: how to check you really understand

Updated 10 November 2026

Contents

  5. The Feynman technique with AI feedback

The Feynman technique is named after physicist Richard Feynman, famous for explaining hard things simply. The point: if you can't explain a topic in simple words, you don't understand it yet.

The technique in four steps

  1. Pick a topic and write its name at the top of a page.
  2. Explain it in simple words, as if to a friend who knows nothing about it. No terms you can't unpack.
  3. Find the gaps. Where you stumbled, skipped a step or hid behind a clever word — that's where understanding is missing. Go back to the source and work it out.
  4. Simplify and add an example. Rewrite the explanation shorter and add an everyday example.

Why it works

When you explain, you don't recognise a ready answer — you build one, which trains memory and understanding much harder (the generation effect and self-explanation). Explaining also breaks the illusion of explanatory depth: in a study by Rozenblit and Keil (2002), people confidently rated how well they understood a zipper or a flush toilet, and their confidence dropped sharply once they tried to explain it step by step.

An example

Topic: "why rereading helps less than recalling". First attempt: "Well, recalling activates memory better." "Activates" explains nothing — that's a gap. After working it out: "When you reread, the text feels familiar and we confuse familiarity with knowledge. When you recall without hints, the brain retrieves the information itself, and that effort strengthens memory. This is called the testing effect."

Where the technique fails

Its main weakness is no feedback. Explaining to yourself, it's easy to miss an error or an important idea: you don't know what you don't know. The technique works better when someone checks your explanation — a teacher, a colleague or the source.

The Feynman technique with AI feedback

In Chompbook every lesson ends with an open question — a small Feynman exercise. You answer in your own words, and AI checks the answer against your source and the lesson's key ideas: a score out of 10, what worked, what's missing and how to improve, plus a model answer. What you missed comes back in later lessons.

Without AI you can compare your answer with the model answer yourself — lessons work offline too.

FAQ

Do I have to explain out loud?

No. Writing works — it even makes gaps easier to spot and compare with the source.

Does the Feynman technique work for humanities?

Yes. It works for any ideas, from economics and history to psychology. An everyday example matters most for abstract topics.

How long does one topic take?

Usually 10–20 minutes for a small topic or chapter. Short and regular is better.
