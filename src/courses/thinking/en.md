# think-01 | Arguments | Claim, argument, evidence
## What an argument is made of
A **claim** is what someone holds to be true: "Remote work boosts productivity." An **argument** is a claim together with reasons for it. **Evidence** is the facts the reasons rest on: data, studies, checkable observations.

Critical thinking starts with a simple question: **"Why should I believe this?"** If there are no reasons or evidence behind a claim, it's an opinion, not an argument.

## The strength of evidence
Evidence comes in different strengths. Roughly from weak to strong:
- one person's story ("my grandad smoked and lived to 90");
- an opinion without a source;
- the opinion of an expert in their field;
- systematic data and studies;
- consistent results from many independent studies.

One vivid story sticks better than statistics, but says far less about the whole picture.

!key The key question: "Why should I believe this?"
!key A personal story is the weakest evidence for general conclusions.
!key Strongest of all: consistent data from many independent studies.

# think-02 | Arguments | Logical fallacies, part 1
## Errors in reasoning
A **logical fallacy** is a move that looks like an argument but doesn't actually support the conclusion. Knowing them helps you spot them in others — and in yourself.

## Three common fallacies
- **Ad hominem**: attacking the person instead of the argument. "What does he know about economics, he can't even drive."
- **Straw man**: distorting the other side's position into one that's easy to attack. — "Let's cut the advertising budget." — "So you want nobody to ever hear about us?"
- **False dilemma**: presenting only two options when there are more. "You're either with us or against us"; "either we fire half the department or the company closes."

How to respond: bring the conversation back to the point ("Let's discuss the argument itself"), clarify your position ("I'm suggesting a cut, not cancelling it") and ask about other options.

!key A fallacy looks like an argument but doesn't support the conclusion.
!key Ad hominem attacks the person, not their point.
!key A straw man argues against a distorted version of a position.
!key A false dilemma hides the other options.

?q:e Which fallacy is this: "Don't listen to her views on taxes — she's just a student"?
+ Ad hominem
- False dilemma
- Straw man

= The person is judged instead of their point.

?q:m — "We should work from home more often." — "So you're suggesting we never come to the office or talk to the team?" Which fallacy is this?
- Ad hominem
+ Straw man
- Appeal to the majority
- False cause
= The position was distorted into an extreme so it's easier to attack.

?q:m "Either we launch the product this month or we lose the market forever." What's wrong here?
- Nothing, it's correct
- It's ad hominem
+ It's a false dilemma: there are other options — a partial launch, a pilot, a one-month delay
- It's sound statistics
= The choice is artificially narrowed to two extremes.

?q:h What's the best way to respond to a straw man in a work discussion?

+ Calmly clarify your actual position and return to the point
- Agree with the distorted version
= Clarifying brings the discussion back to the real proposal.

?r:m What is a false dilemma?
+ Presenting a situation as if there were only two options
- A calculation error
- An attack on a person
- An appeal to authority
= In reality there are usually more options.

?r:e What does an ad hominem target?
- The arguments
+ The person instead of their argument
- The statistics
- The sources
= Hence the Latin name: "to the person".

# think-03 | Arguments | Logical fallacies, part 2
## Four more traps
- **Appeal to the majority**: "Everyone does it, so it's right." Popularity isn't proof.
- **Appeal to authority** outside their field: a famous actor advertising a medicine. An expert's opinion carries weight **in their own field** and when it rests on data.
- **Slippery slope**: "If we allow people to be 5 minutes late, soon everyone will turn up at lunchtime." A chain of consequences is declared inevitable without evidence.
- **Post hoc** ("after it, therefore because of it"): "I wore my lucky jumper and the team won." One event happening after another doesn't mean it was caused by it.

## How to check
Ask: is there data, not just popularity? Is the expert speaking in their field? How likely is each link in the chain? What other causes could have led to the result?

!key An idea's popularity doesn't prove it right.
!key An authority's opinion counts only in their field and when backed by data.
!key A slippery slope declares a chain of consequences inevitable without evidence.
!key "After" doesn't mean "because of".

?q:e "Millions of people drink this slimming tea, so it works." Which fallacy is this?

- Slippery slope
- Straw man
- False dilemma
= Popularity doesn't prove effectiveness.

- It's an ad hominem

= Other causes need to be ruled out.

+ Many people can be wrong at the same time

# think-04 | Thinking traps | Cognitive biases
## Fast but crooked rules
The brain saves effort by using quick rules of thumb — **heuristics**. They usually help, but they fail systematically in predictable situations. These errors are called **cognitive biases** — the psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky described them in detail.

## Three important biases
- **Confirmation bias**: we notice and seek out what confirms our view and miss what contradicts it.
- **Anchoring**: the first number we hear influences our estimate. If a seller starts with a high price, the "discount" seems generous.
- **Availability heuristic**: we judge how likely events are by how easily examples come to mind. After news of a plane crash, flying seems more dangerous, although statistically planes are one of the safest ways to travel.

## What helps
- Deliberately look for arguments **against** your position.
- Make your own estimate before hearing someone else's number.
- Ask: "How often does this actually happen, according to data?"

!key Cognitive biases are systematic errors of quick mental rules.
!key Confirmation bias: we see what confirms our view.
!key Anchoring: the first number sways the estimate.
!key Availability: vivid examples seem frequent; check the data.

- An error when confirming a payment

= We look for "evidence" for what we already believe.

# think-05 | Thinking traps | Correlation and causation
## Together doesn't mean because
**Correlation** means two measures change together. **Causation** means one causes the other. A classic example: ice-cream sales and drownings rise together. Ice cream doesn't drown people — both rise in summer because of the heat. This is a **hidden third cause**.

Correlation can have other explanations too:
- **reverse causation**: not "sport makes people healthy" but healthy people do more sport (in fact both directions are at work);
- **coincidence**: with lots of data, chance matches are inevitable.

## Sampling errors
- **Selection bias**: an online poll asking whether people use the internet.
- **Survivorship bias**: studying only the successful ones. During the Second World War the statistician Abraham Wald suggested reinforcing not the places on returning planes that had bullet holes, but the places that had none — planes hit there didn't come back.

The best way to test causation is an **experiment with random assignment** to groups: that way third causes are spread evenly.

!key Correlation: measures move together; causation: one causes the other.
!key Look for a hidden third cause, reverse causation and coincidence.
!key Survivorship bias: studying only the successful leads to wrong conclusions.
!key A randomised experiment tests causation best.

?q:e Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together. Why?
- Ice cream causes drownings
+ Both rise in summer because of the heat — a hidden third cause
- Drownings increase ice-cream sales

= Correlation doesn't mean causation.

?q:m What is survivorship bias?
+ Drawing conclusions only from the "surviving" cases, without seeing those that dropped out
- An error in medical statistics

= For example, studying only successful start-ups.

?q:m A poll on a travel website shows that 90% of people travel abroad every year. What's the problem?
- There's no problem
- The percentage is too small
+ The sample is biased: the site is visited mostly by travellers
- The poll should have been run at night
= The sample doesn't represent everyone.

?q:h A company notices that staff who took a sales course sell more. What would most reliably prove that the course is the reason?

+ Randomly assigning similar employees to take the course or not, and comparing the results
- Calculating how much the course cost
- Sending only the best salespeople on the course
= Randomisation rules out hidden causes — for example, that the most motivated people sign up.

?r:m What did Abraham Wald suggest about armouring planes?

- Reinforce the places where returning planes had holes
- Not reinforce the planes at all

= Planes with holes in those places were the ones that didn't return.

?r:e How is correlation different from causation?
- It isn't
+ Correlation is changing together, causation is one factor causing another
- Causation is a weak correlation
- Correlation only exists in economics
= Changing together doesn't tell you what causes what.

# think-06 | Information | How to check sources
## Five questions for a source
1. **Who is the author?** Do they know the subject? Is there a conflict of interest?
2. **Where does the data come from?** Is there a link to the primary source — a study, document, statistics?
3. **When was it published?** Is the information out of date?
4. **Why was it written?** To inform, to sell, to persuade, to provoke outrage?
5. **Do other independent sources confirm it?**

## Lateral reading
Professional fact-checkers don't study a dubious website in depth; they immediately **open new tabs** and look at what others say about the site and the author. This is called **lateral reading**. Research by the Stanford History Education Group showed that fact-checkers spot unreliable sources this way faster and more accurately than historians and students.

## Red flags
An emotional headline, "doctors are hiding this", no author or date, links that lead in circles back to the same site, a photo with no source. A photo can be checked with a **reverse image search** — it often turns out to be old or from another country.

!key Five questions: who, where from, when, why, who else confirms it.
!key Lateral reading: check what others say about the source.
!key Red flags: emotion, "they're hiding it", no author or date.
!key Check photos with a reverse image search.

# think-07 | Information | Numbers that mislead
## Relative and absolute risk
"Halves the risk" sounds impressive. But if the risk was 2 in 10,000 and became 1 in 10,000, the absolute reduction is just 1 case per 10,000 people. Always ask: **"What was it and what is it now, in absolute numbers?"**

## Base rates
A test for a rare disease is 99% accurate. The disease affects 1 person in 1,000. Of 1,000 people tested, the one who is ill will almost certainly test positive — but about 10 healthy people will also get a false positive. So with a positive test, the chance of having the disease is roughly 1 in 11, not 99%. Ignoring the base rate is one of the most common mistakes.

## Other traps
- **Small samples**: "4 out of 5 dentists recommend" — which five?
- **Cherry-picked periods**: a chart starting "from the low point" shows growth that doesn't exist over a longer stretch.
- **The mean instead of the median** when there are outliers.
- **Numbers without comparison**: "1,000 incidents" — is that a lot or a little for a city that size?

!key Ask for absolute numbers: "what was it and what is it now".
!key Mind the base rate: a rare event produces many false alarms.
!key Small samples and convenient periods distort the picture.
!key A number without comparison means nothing.

?q:e An ad says: "This drug halves the risk." What should you ask first?

- Who makes the packaging?
- How many people have bought the drug?
= "Halves" may mean going from 2 to 1 in 10,000.

# think-08 | Information | How to argue and change your mind
## Arguing to understand
The aim of a good argument is not to win but to **get closer to the truth**. Useful techniques:
- **Steelman** — the opposite of a straw man: state the other side's position in its strongest form, so that they would agree with it. Then respond.
- **Questions instead of attacks**: "What would change your mind?", "What data are you relying on?".
- Separate **facts** ("sales fell 10%") from **values** ("keeping people matters more than profit") — they are argued about differently.

## Confidence in percentages
Beliefs don't have to be split into "believe / don't believe". It helps to think in probabilities: "I'm 70% sure." New data shifts confidence little by little. Admitting "I was wrong" is a sign of strong thinking, not weakness.

## Course summary
Ask for reasons and evidence, notice fallacies and biases, check sources and numbers, argue honestly. Critical thinking is a skill, trained like any other: regularly and on real examples.
