# money-01 | The basics | Budget: where the money goes
## Tracking comes first
A budget starts not with restrictions but with **understanding**: how much comes in and where it goes. For a month or two, record all your spending — in your bank's app, a spreadsheet or a notebook. Many people are surprised by "small" spending: coffee, delivery and subscriptions add up to a noticeable sum.

It's handy to split spending into **needs** (housing, utilities, food, transport, debt payments) and **wants** (cafés, entertainment, treats).

## The 50/30/20 guideline
A popular scheme from the book "All Your Worth" (2005) by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi: about **50%** of after-tax income for needs, **30%** for wants, **20%** for savings and paying off debt early.

It's a guideline, not a law: with high rent the proportions will differ. The main thing is that savings are a regular line in the budget, not "whatever is left".

## Pay yourself first
Put part of your income aside **on payday itself** — with an automatic transfer. That way your savings don't depend on willpower at the end of the month.

# money-02 | The basics | An emergency fund
## Why you need one
An **emergency fund** is money for the unexpected: losing your job, illness, an urgent repair. Without it, any trouble turns into debt at a high interest rate.

A common guideline is **3–6 months of essential expenses**. People with irregular income (freelancers, seasonal work) or dependants should aim for the upper end and beyond.

## Where to keep it
An emergency fund should be:
- **accessible** — you can withdraw it within a day or two without losses;
- **safe** — no risk of losing part of it;
- **separate** from your everyday card, so you don't spend it by accident.

Usually that's a savings account or a deposit you can withdraw from. Shares and cryptocurrency don't fit: when you need the money, they may be down.

## Where to start
If 3 months feels out of reach, start with a first goal — say, one month of expenses — and save regularly. You build the emergency fund before you start investing.

# money-03 | Debt | Loans and debt
## The full cost
A loan is a way to buy now and pay more later. Compare loans not by the advertised rate but by the **total cost of credit** — the annual rate including all mandatory fees: insurance, commissions, service charges. Different countries call it different things: APR, effective annual rate and so on.

## Credit cards
Many cards have a **grace period**: if you pay the balance in full before it ends, no interest is charged. But if you only make the minimum payment, the debt shrinks very slowly, and card interest rates are usually high. Cash withdrawals on a credit card are often excluded from the grace period and cost more.

## Paying off several debts
- **Avalanche**: pay off the debt with the highest rate first — you pay less interest overall.
- **Snowball**: pay off the smallest debt first — quick wins keep you motivated.

Either way, keep making the minimum payments on the other debts without missing any. Taking a new loan to close an old one only makes sense if the new one really is cheaper in total cost.

# money-04 | Growing money | Compound interest
## Interest on interest
**Compound interest** means income is earned not only on the original amount but also on the interest already earned. Example: 10,000 at 10% a year with compounding. After one year — 11,000, after two — 12,100, after 10 years — about **25,900**. Without compounding it would be 20,000.

## Time is your main ally
The earlier you start, the stronger the effect: the last years bring more than the first. A small regular amount from a young age can overtake large investments started late.

The **rule of 72** helps estimate how many years it takes for money to double: divide 72 by the annual rate in percent. At 8% — about 9 years, at 12% — about 6.

## It works both ways
Compound interest works against you too: an unpaid high-interest debt grows just as fast. That's why it's wise to pay off expensive debt before you start investing.

# money-05 | Growing money | Inflation and real return
## Money loses value
**Inflation** is the rise in prices over time. If prices rose by 8% over a year, the same amount buys less. Money "under the mattress" loses purchasing power.

## Real return
What matters isn't the percentage you're promised but what remains **after inflation**. Roughly: real return ≈ nominal rate − inflation. A deposit at 6% with 8% inflation gives about **−2%** in real terms: there's more money in the account, but it buys less.

Also take into account tax on income, if your country has it — it reduces the real result too.

## What follows
- Compare returns with inflation, not with zero.
- Long-term goals (retirement, children's education) need investments that on average beat inflation — but they come with risk (next lesson).
- The emergency fund is the exception: safety matters more for it than beating inflation.

# money-06 | Growing money | Risk and diversification
## Return and risk go together
The higher the expected return, the higher the risk — swings in value and the chance of losing part of your money. There are no investments that are at once **high-yield, safe and available at any moment**. If you're promised all of it, "guaranteed", that's a red flag.

## Don't put all your eggs in one basket
**Diversification** means spreading money across different assets: companies, industries, countries, types of investment. If one investment falls, the others soften the blow. A simple way to get broad diversification is **index funds**, which buy many companies at once.

## Time horizon and patience
- Money you'll need in a year or two shouldn't go into risky assets.
- For goals 10+ years away, swings are easier to ride out — there's time to recover.
- The main mistake is panic-selling in a fall and buying in a frenzy at the peak.
- Invest only in what you understand, and watch the fees: they eat into returns.

# money-07 | Protection | Scammers and pyramid schemes
## How scammers work
Almost every scheme plays on emotions: **fear** ("money is being taken from your card"), **greed** ("30% a month") or **urgency** ("decide within the hour"). The goal is to make you act fast without thinking.

Typical tricks:
- a call "from the bank's security team" or "from the police" asking you to move money to a "safe account";
- a request to tell them an **SMS code**, the card's CVV or a password;
- a message "from a friend" asking for an urgent loan — from a hacked account;
- fake shop and bank websites with look-alike addresses.

## Pyramid schemes
A pyramid pays "returns" to old members out of new members' money. Signs: guaranteed high returns, no clear source of profit, bonuses for inviting friends, pressure to "get in now". Sooner or later the flow of new money dries up, and most members lose what they put in.

## Rules of protection
- A bank never asks for SMS codes and never moves money to "safe accounts".
- Hang up and call your bank back on the number on the back of your card.
- Check requests from "friends" with a phone call.
- Don't rush: urgency is a scammer's main tool.

# money-08 | Protection | Financial goals and a plan
## Goals with numbers and dates
"Save more" isn't a goal. A good financial goal is **specific**: an amount, a deadline and a purpose. "20,000 for a down payment in 2 years" becomes a clear monthly contribution — about 833 a month, not counting interest.

## The order of steps
A typical sequence that suits most people:
1. Track spending and make a budget.
2. Minimum payments on all debts, with none missed.
3. A starter emergency fund — at least one month of expenses.
4. Pay off expensive debt.
5. A full emergency fund — 3–6 months.
6. Long-term goals and diversified investments.

## Automation and review
- Set up automatic transfers to your goals on payday.
- Every few months, review your budget and goals: life changes.
- Before big decisions (a mortgage, investments, insurance), study the terms and, if needed, consult a specialist who doesn't earn a commission from selling you the product.
