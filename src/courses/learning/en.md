# learn-01 | How memory works | Why we forget
## Three stages of memory
To learn something, the brain goes through three stages: it **encodes** the information (pays attention and understands it), **stores** it, and later **retrieves** it — recalls it when needed.

Most learning problems are not about a "bad memory". They come from weak encoding (we read half-distracted) or from hardly ever practising retrieval.

## The forgetting curve
In 1885 the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus memorised nonsense syllables and measured how much he still remembered over time. Forgetting turned out to be fastest in the first hours and days and to slow down afterwards. This pattern is called the **forgetting curve**.

The good news: every time you successfully recall material, forgetting slows down. So what matters is not how many times you have *seen* a text, but how many times you have *recalled* it.

# learn-02 | How memory works | Recall instead of rereading
## The testing effect
In 2006 the psychologists Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke compared two groups of students. One group reread a text several times; the other read it and then tried to recall what it said. A week later the second group remembered noticeably more. This is called the **testing effect**, or retrieval practice.

Trying to remember is not just a check of what you know — it is the training itself. Even a wrong answer helps: after it, the correct answer sticks better.

## The illusion of knowing
Rereading feels good: the text looks familiar, so it seems you know it. This is the **fluency illusion** — recognising a text and recalling it on your own are different things.

Simple ways to practise retrieval:
- close the text and retell the main points in your own words;
- answer questions before rereading;
- make flashcards: a question on one side, the answer on the other.

That is exactly why every Chompbook lesson has questions and a review.

# learn-03 | How memory works | Spaced repetition
## The spacing effect
If you review material with gaps in between instead of all at once, you remember it much longer. This is the **spacing effect** — one of the most reliable findings in learning science.

An hour of study split into six 10-minute sessions on different days gives more than the same hour in one evening. Cramming before an exam helps you pass tomorrow, but a month later almost nothing is left.

## Growing gaps
**Expanding intervals** work best: review after a day, then after three days, a week, two weeks and so on. Each review comes a little "late" — you have almost forgotten but manage to recall, and that very effort strengthens memory.

The Leitner flashcard system is built on this: correct cards move to a box you review less often, mistakes go back to the start. Chompbook does the same automatically: mistakes come back the next day, right answers after 3, 7, 14, 30 and 60 days.

# learn-04 | Techniques for deep understanding | Interleaving topics
## Blocks versus interleaving
We usually study in **blocks**: first 20 problems of one type, then 20 of another. It feels productive — each problem gets easier. But in real life problems come mixed, and you also have to figure out which method to use.

With **interleaving**, problems of different types are mixed: A, B, C, A, C, B. In Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor's maths studies (2007), students who practised mixed problems did noticeably better on the final test, even though practice felt harder for them.

## When it works
Interleaving is especially useful when topics are similar and easy to confuse: kinds of charts, grammatical tenses, formulas, painters' styles. The brain learns to notice the differences, not just to repeat one technique.

Important: interleave topics you already know a little. Brand-new material is better studied separately first and mixed afterwards.

# learn-05 | Techniques for deep understanding | Explain and connect
## Explaining in your own words
We remember better what we connect to things we already know. **Elaboration** means asking yourself "why is this so?" and "how does it relate to what I know?".

The **Feynman technique** works in a similar way: explain the topic in simple words, as if to a beginner. Wherever the explanation stumbles, there is a gap — go back to the source, work it out and explain again.

## Words plus pictures
Another technique is **dual coding**: combining text with a diagram, table or drawing. Two forms of one idea give memory two routes to it.

## The learning-styles myth
The popular idea that everyone should learn in "their own style" — visual learners with pictures, auditory learners by ear — is not supported by research (a review by Pashler and colleagues, 2008). Choose the format to fit the **material**: diagrams for processes, examples for rules, practice for skills.

# learn-06 | The study habit | Attention and habit
## One task at a time
Switching between tasks is expensive: after every interruption it takes time to get back in. Even a phone lying face down on the desk can take some of your attention. A simple rule: during a session, one material, notifications off, phone in another room or on "do not disturb".

## Small daily portions
A habit is held up not by willpower but by **regularity and an easy start**. Ten minutes every day works better than two hours on Sundays — both for memory (spacing!) and for the habit.

An **"if–then" plan** (implementation intention) helps: "If I sit down on the morning train, then I open a lesson." Research by Peter Gollwitzer shows that such specific plans raise the chance of actually doing what you intended.

## Don't break the chain
A streak of study days is motivating: you don't want to break it. But a missed day is not a failure. The main thing is not to miss two days in a row. That's what freezes and rest days in Chompbook are for.

# learn-07 | The study habit | Sleep, movement and breaks
## Sleep consolidates memory
During sleep the brain processes and consolidates what we learned during the day — this is called **memory consolidation**. Lack of sleep hurts learning twice: attention is worse today and the material is consolidated worse for tomorrow. The night before an exam is better spent sleeping than cramming.

## Breaks and movement
Attention can't be held forever. Short breaks help you carry on with the same focus. A popular format is the **Pomodoro technique**: 25 minutes of work, 5 minutes of break. It isn't a law of nature, just a handy frame — find your own rhythm.

During a break it's better to get up and move than to scroll your feed: a walk or a stretch gives attention a rest, while social media takes it again. Regular physical activity is good for the brain and mood in general.

# learn-08 | The study habit | Your learning plan
## A goal you can check
"Learn English" is not a goal, it's a dream. A good learning goal is **specific and checkable**: "In three months I'll be able to hold a 15-minute work call in English." Then it's clear what to practise and how to tell that it worked.

Break the big goal into steps the size of one session. Each step should end with an action: answer questions, do an exercise, apply it at work.

## Sources and checking
Pick 1–3 good sources and don't jump between a dozen courses. In Chompbook you can create your own skillset and add books, articles and links — the app will cut them into daily portions with questions.

Check yourself regularly: intermediate and final tests show what has stuck and what needs reviewing. Once a week ask yourself: what worked, what got in the way, what to change.

## Putting it all together
Recall instead of rereading, review at growing intervals, interleave similar topics, explain in your own words, study a little every day and get enough sleep. That is evidence-based learning.
